    Section 1.3 Plane Jacobian conjecture and small examples

    Let \(P,Q,R,S\in K[x,y]\text{.}\) Since \(f_{P,Q}\circ f_{R,S} = f_{P(R,S),Q(R,S)}\text{,}\) the chain rule says that
    🔗
    \begin{equation} \begin{pmatrix} P(R,S)_x & P(R,S)_y \\ Q(R,S)_x & Q(R,S)_y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} P_x(R,S) & P_y(R,S) \\ Q_x(R,S) & Q_y(R,S) \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} R_x & R_y \\ S_x & S_y \end{pmatrix}.\tag{1.3.1} \end{equation}
    Let \(\varphi\colon K[x,y]\to K[x,y]\) be an endomorphism of algebras and write \(R := \varphi(x)\) and \(S := \varphi(y)\text{.}\) Then equality (1.3.1) becomes
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{pmatrix} \varphi(P)_x & \varphi(P)_y \\ \varphi(Q)_x & \varphi(Q)_y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \varphi(P_x) & \varphi(P_y) \\ \varphi(Q_x) & \varphi(Q_y) \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \varphi(x)_x & \varphi(x)_y \\ \varphi(y)_x & \varphi(y)_y \end{pmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    since \(\varphi(P) = P(R,S)\text{,}\) etcetera. Consequently
    🔗
    \begin{equation} [\varphi(P),\varphi(Q)] = \varphi([P,Q])[\varphi(x),\varphi(y)]\quad\text{for all } P,Q\in K[x,y].\tag{1.3.2} \end{equation}

    Definition 1.3.1. Polynomial inverse.

    Let \(f = f_{P,Q}\) be a polynomial map. We say that f has a polynomial inverse if \(f\) is invertible and there exist \(R,S\in K[x,y]\) such that \(f^{-1} = f_{R,S}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark 1.3.2.

    Clearly \(f_{R,S}\) is inverse of \(f_{P,Q}\) if and only if
    🔗
    \begin{equation} R(P,Q) = P(R,S) = x \quad\text{and}\quad S(P,Q) = Q(R,S) = y\tag{1.3.3} \end{equation}
    🔗

    Remark 1.3.3.

    If \(f = f_{P,Q}\) has a polynomial inverse \(f_{R,S}\text{,}\) then, by (1.3.1)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{pmatrix} R_x(P,Q) & R_y(P,Q) \\ S_x(P,Q) & S_y(P,Q) \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} P_x & P_y \\ Q_x & Q_y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    and so
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{J}(R,S)|_{(P,Q)} \operatorname{J}(P,Q) = 1. \end{equation*}
    Consequently \([P,Q] = \operatorname{J}(P,Q) \in K^{\times}\text{,}\) since the only invertible polynomials are the non zero constants.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    The plane Jacobian conjecture states that if \([P,Q] \in K^{\times}\text{,}\) then the polynomial map \(f_{P,Q}\colon K^2 \to K^2\) has a polynomial inverse. In other words, the Jacobian conjecture states that the necessary condition for the invertibility of \(f_{P,Q}\text{,}\) obtained above, is sufficient.
    🔗

    Definition 1.3.4. Jacobian pair.

    A Jacobian pair is a pair \((P,Q)\text{,}\) of polynomials \(P,Q\in K[x,y]\text{,}\) such that \([P,Q] \in K^{\times}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    For a pair of polynomials \((P,Q)\) in two variables with coefficients in \(K\text{,}\) we let \(K[P,Q]\) and \(\varphi_{P,Q}\) denote the \(K\)-subalgebra of \(K[x,y]\) generated by \(P,Q\) and the \(K\)-algebra endomorphism of \(K[x,y]\) defined by \(\varphi_{P,Q}(R) = R(P,Q)\text{,}\) respectively.
    🔗

    Proposition 1.3.5. .

    Let \(P,Q\in K[x,y]\text{.}\) The following assertions are equivalent:
    🔗
    1. \(f_{P,Q}\) has a polynomial inverse.
      🔗
    2. \(\varphi_{P,Q}\) is an automorphism.
      🔗
    3. \(K[P,Q] = K[x,y]\text{.}\)
      🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    By definition
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \varphi_{R,S}\bigl(\varphi_{P,Q}(x)\bigr) = \varphi_{R,S}(P) = P(R,S) \quad\text{and}\quad \varphi_{R,S}\bigl(\varphi_{P,Q}(y)\bigr) = \varphi_{R,S}(Q) = Q(R,S). \end{equation*}
    Therefore, \(\varphi_{R,S} \circ \varphi_{P,Q} = \varphi_{P(R,S),Q(R,S)}\text{.}\) Similarly \(\varphi_{P,Q} \circ \varphi_{R,S} = \varphi_{R(P,Q),S(P,Q)}\text{,}\) and so \(\varphi_{R,S}\) is the inverse of \(\varphi_{P,Q}\) if and only if identities (1.3.3) are fulfilled. This proves that items 1 and 2 are equivalent. Clearly, 2 implies 3. It remains to prove that 3 implies 2. But this is true, since 3 says that \(\varphi_{P,Q}\) is surjective, and each surjective algebra endomorphism of \(K[x,y]\) is an isomorphism, because \(K[x,y]\) is Noetherian.
    🔗
    🔗
    By Proposition 1.3.5, version 1 of the Jacobian conjecture is equivalent to the following ones:
    🔗
    Plane Jacobian conjecture (version 2). If \([P,Q]\in K^{\times}\text{,}\) then \(\varphi_{P,Q}\) is an automorphism of \(K[x,y]\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Plane Jacobian conjecture (version 3). If \([P,Q]\in K^{\times}\text{,}\) then \(K[P,Q] = K[x,y]\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Remark 1.3.6.

    Clearly, for each polynomial in one variable \(p\) with coefficients in \(K\text{,}\) the maps \(\varphi_{x,y+p(x)}\) and \(\varphi_{x+p(y),y}\) are invertible (with inverses \(\varphi_{x,y-p(x)}\) and \(\varphi_{x-p(y),y}\text{,}\) respectively). These maps are called elementary automorphisms .
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 1.3.7.

    Let \(P := x^4 + 2x^2y + y^2 + x\) and \(Q := x^2 + y\text{.}\) Notice that
    \begin{align*} [P,Q] &= [x^4,x^2] + [2x^2y,x^2] + [y^2,x^2] + [x,x^2] + [x^4,y] + [2x^2y,y] + [y^2,y] + [x,y]\\ &= 2x^2[y,x^2] + [y^2,x^2] + [x^4,y] + y[2x^2,y] + [x,y]\\ &= -4x^3[x,y] - 4xy[x,y] + 4x^3[x,y] + 4xy[x,y] + [x,y]\\ &= 1. \end{align*}
    As \(\varphi_{P,Q} = \varphi_{x,x^2+y} \circ \varphi_{y^2+x,y}\text{,}\) Remark 1.3.6 implies that \(\varphi_{P,Q}\) is an automorphism.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proposition 1.3.8.

    If \((P,Q)\) is a Jacobian pair and \(\varphi \in \operatorname{Aut}(K[x,y])\text{,}\) then \((\varphi(P),\varphi(Q))\) also is a Jacobian pair.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    By the equivalence between items 1 and 2 of Proposition 1.3.5 and Remark 1.3.3, we know that \([\varphi(x),\varphi(y)] \in K^{\times}\text{.}\) Hence, from (1.3.2), we obtain \([\varphi(P),\varphi(Q)] \in K^{\times}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proposition 1.3.9.

    If \((P,Q)\) is a counterexample to the Jacobian Conjecture and \(\varphi \in \operatorname{Aut}(K[x,y])\text{,}\) then \((\varphi(P),\varphi(Q))\) also is a counterexample to the Jacobian Conjecture.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    By the previous proposition, \([\varphi(P),\varphi(Q)] \in K^{\times}\text{.}\) Set \(R := \varphi(x)\) and \(S := \varphi(y)\text{,}\) so that \(\varphi = \varphi_{R,S}\text{.}\) Since \(\varphi(P) = P(R,S)\) and \(\varphi(Q) = Q(R,S)\text{,}\) we have
    \begin{equation*} f_{\varphi(P),\varphi(Q)} = f_{P(R,S),Q(R,S)} = f_{P,Q} \circ f_{R,S}. \end{equation*}
    Moreover, again by Proposition 1.3.5, we know that \(f_{R,S}\) has a polynomial inverse. Consequently \(f_{\varphi(P),\varphi(Q)}\) has a polynomial inverse if and only if \(f_{P,Q}\) does.
    🔗
    🔗
    From now on we will use Proposition 1.3.9 without explicit mention.
    🔗

    Proposition 1.3.10.

    Let \((P,Q)\) be a Jacobian pair. If
    \begin{equation*} P \in K[x],\quad P \in K[y],\quad Q \in K[x],\quad Q \in K[y]\quad\text{or}\quad \deg(P) + \deg(Q) \le 2, \end{equation*}
    then \(\varphi_{P,Q}\) is an automorphism.
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.
    Assume that \(P \in K[x]\text{,}\) which implies \(P_y = 0\text{.}\) Hence \(P_x Q_y = [P,Q] \in K^{\times}\text{.}\) So, \(P_x, Q_y \in K^{\times}\) and consequently, there exist \(a \in K\) and \(b,c \in K^{\times}\) such that \(P = a + bx\) and \(Q = cy + Q_0(x)\text{.}\) Then, the polynomials
    \begin{equation*} R := \frac{x - a}{b}\quad\text{and}\quad S := \frac{1}{c}\left(y - Q_0\!\left(\frac{x-a}{b}\right)\right) \end{equation*}
    satisfy the conditions \(R(P,Q) = x\text{,}\) \(S(P,Q) = y\text{,}\) \(P(R,S) = x\) and \(Q(R,S) = y\text{;}\) and consequently, \(\varphi_{P,Q}\) is an automorphism. A similar argument shows that a Jacobian pair with \(P \in K[y]\text{,}\) \(Q \in K[x]\) or \(Q \in K[y]\) defines an automorphism. It remains to prove that if \(\deg(P) + \deg(Q) \le 2\text{,}\) then \(\varphi_{P,Q}\) is also an automorphism. If \(\deg(P) = 0\) or \(\deg(Q) = 0\text{,}\) then \([P,Q] = 0\text{,}\) which contradicts the fact that \((P,Q)\) is a Jacobian pair. Thus, necessarily \(\deg(P) = \deg(Q) = 1\text{,}\) and so there exist \(a,b,c,d,e,f \in K\) such that \((b,c) \ne (0,0)\text{,}\) \((e,f) \ne (0,0)\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} P = a + bx + cy\quad\text{and}\quad Q = d + ex + fy. \end{equation*}
    Moreover, \(bf - ec = \bigl| \begin{smallmatrix}b & c \\ e & f \end{smallmatrix}\bigr| = [P,Q] \ne 0\text{.}\) Let
    \begin{equation*} R := \frac{1}{[P,Q]}(cd - af + fx - cy)\quad\text{and}\quad S := \frac{1}{[P,Q]}(ae - bd - ex + by). \end{equation*}
    A straightforward computation shows that \(R(P,Q) = x\text{,}\) \(S(P,Q) = y\text{,}\) \(P(R,S) = x\) and \(Q(R,S) = y\text{,}\) and thus \(\varphi_{P,Q}\) is an automorphism.
    🔗
    🔗
    A technique we will frequently use in these notes is the “reduction” of the support. Given a pair \((P,Q)\) of polynomials in \(K[x,y]\) and a problem involving \((P,Q)\text{,}\) the idea is to apply an automorphism \(\varphi\) of \(K[x,y]\) such that:
    🔗
    • Solving the problem for \((\varphi(P),\varphi(Q))\) implies solving it for \((P,Q)\text{,}\)
      🔗
    • the supports of \(\varphi(P)\) and \(\varphi(Q)\) make the problem easier to solve for these polynomials.
      🔗
    Many times the modification of the support involves a true reduction of the size of the supports of \(P\) and \(Q\) in some sense (cardinal, area, etc.). For instance, suppose that \(\varphi_{P,Q}\) is invertible and that we want to find \(\varphi_{P,Q}^{-1}\text{.}\) Since
    \begin{equation*} \varphi_{P,Q}^{-1}(P) = x\quad\text{and}\quad \varphi_{P,Q}^{-1}(Q) = y, \end{equation*}
    an idea is to take an automorphism \(\varphi_1\) that reduces the supports of \(P\) and \(Q\text{;}\) then, an automorphism \(\varphi_2\) that reduces the supports of \(\varphi_1(P)\) and \(\varphi_1(Q)\text{;}\) and so on. Let us see how this technique works in a concrete case: take \(P := x^4 + 2x^2y + y^2 + x\) and \(Q := x^2 + y\text{.}\) We saw in Example 1.3.7 that \(\varphi_{P,Q}\) is invertible. In order to find \(\varphi_{P,Q}^{-1}\text{,}\) we first apply the elementary automorphism \(\varphi_1 := \varphi_{x,y-x^2}\text{,}\) and we obtain \(\varphi_1(P) = x + y^2\) and \(\varphi_1(Q) = y\) (see Figure 1.3.11).
    🔗
    Then, we apply the elementary automorphism \(\varphi_2 := \varphi_{x-y^2,y}\text{.}\) Clearly
    \begin{equation*} \varphi_2(\varphi_1(P)) = x \quad\text{and}\quad \varphi_2(\varphi_1(Q)) = y, \end{equation*}
    and so \(\varphi_2 \circ \varphi_1 = \varphi_{P,Q}^{-1}\text{.}\) By the way, it is also possible to find \(\varphi_{P,Q}^{-1}\) using Remark 1.3.6 and Example 1.3.7.
    🔗
    Later we will also use the support reduction technique in some extensions of the polynomial ring in two variables.
